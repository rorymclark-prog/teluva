## 0. Why a DPIA is required (Art. 35(3))

A DPIA is mandatory here, not optional, because the processing meets **multiple** independent triggers under Art. 35(3) GDPR and the EDPB's nine-criteria guidance (WP248 rev.01):

1. **Special-category data on a large scale of data types** — Art. 9 health data (blood group, allergies, medications, chronic conditions, vaccinations, surgeries, organ-donor status, family medical history, growth history) processed alongside government ID numbers.
2. **Data concerning vulnerable data subjects** — children (Art. 8 territory), whose data is entered and managed by a parent rather than the child.
3. **Systematic and extensive processing involving new/AI technology** — an AI assistant that reads free text and OCRs photographed identity/medical documents, plus AI image generation (avatar restyling).
4. **Evaluation or scoring is not present**, but the combination of (1)+(2)+(3) alone clears the EDPB's "two or more criteria" threshold, and Austria's DSB blacklist (per existing project research, see `legal-compliance-research-2026-07.md` §5) separately lists health-data processing and processing of children's data as DPIA-mandatory categories.

Conclusion: **DPIA required. This draft is the first pass.**

## 1. Purpose and necessity/proportionality of the processing

### 1.1 What the processing is for
Family Vault exists so that **one parent/user**, on behalf of their own family, can store, organise, and retrieve records that are otherwise scattered across paper, photos, and memory: identity documents, government ID numbers, health information, school details, financial reference numbers, home credentials (wifi/door codes/passwords), and travel documents. A secondary layer of purposes (AI assistant, AI avatars, reminders, time-limited carer sharing) is built on top of that core record-store.

### 1.2 Necessity
- The controller's product thesis is that fragmented family-admin information (health, ID, school, financial reference data) causes real harm when it *isn't* organised — missed medication doses, expired documents, lost passwords, carers without emergency information. Consolidating it into one structured store is the stated mechanism for reducing that harm.
- Each data category maps to a named use case in the fact set above (identity → travel/ID renewal tracking, health → allergy/medication recall and carer safety, financial references → record-keeping, credentials → household access). None of the categories currently look purely decorative, but this has **not been independently tested feature-by-feature** — see Outstanding Action 7.4.

### 1.3 Proportionality
- The processing is **user-initiated and user-scoped**: nothing is collected passively or from third parties; every field is something the family member (or their parent, for a child) chose to type or upload.
- Special-category (health) data and AI processing are gated behind **explicit, off-by-default consent** (§4.3, §4.4) rather than being on by default — this is a proportionality control, not just a legal-basis control.
- The carer-share feature deliberately narrows the data set to a server-enforced whitelist of "carer-safe" fields rather than exposing the full family record — a proportionality-by-design measure for third-party sharing (§2.3, §6.4).
- Counter-consideration the controller should weigh honestly: a single app aggregating passport numbers, national ID numbers, SV numbers, health history, and door/wifi codes for an entire family is a **high-value, high-harm-on-breach** data set precisely because it is consolidated. Proportionality here rests heavily on the security measures in §6 actually holding — this DPIA does not independently verify that they do (see §7).

## 2. Description of the processing

### 2.1 Data subjects
- The account-holding parent/user (admin role).
- Other adult family members added as `member` role.
- **Children**, added under a `child` role. Children's data is created and managed by the parent; a child-role account can never enable the AI features (server-enforced, per the fact set).
- Third parties are not data subjects of stored records but may briefly view a **carer-safe subset** via a time-limited share link (a temporary access relationship, not a stored profile).

### 2.2 Data categories (grouped by sensitivity)

| Group | Fields | GDPR classification |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Names, nicknames, DOB, nationality, place of birth, gender, languages | Ordinary personal data (Art. 6); DOB/gender of a minor add Art. 8 sensitivity |
| Government IDs | Passport no., national ID, residence-permit no., driver's licence no., Austrian e-card/SV no., tax no. | Ordinary personal data, but high-harm identity-theft category |
| Contact | Home address, phone, email | Ordinary personal data |
| **Health (Art. 9)** | Blood group, allergies, medications & emergency medication, chronic conditions, vaccinations, surgeries, organ-donor status, family medical history, children's growth history (height/weight over time), recurring care schedules (dentist/check-up dates) | **Special category — Art. 9** |
| Education | School, grade, teacher name & contact | Ordinary personal data; concerns a minor |
| Financial references | Bank name/IBAN/BIC, insurance policy numbers, benefit references | Ordinary personal data (reference data only — **no payment processing occurs in-app**) |
| Credentials | Passwords, wifi keys, door codes, other digital-account passwords | Ordinary personal data but functions as a security credential — treated at elevated protection level (encrypted at rest, §6.4) |
| Documents/scans | Uploaded images/PDFs: passports, Meldezettel, medical letters, school reports, ID cards | Mixed — frequently contains Art. 9 data (medical letters) and government-ID data within a single image |
| Photos & AI avatars | Family photos; AI-restyled avatar images | Ordinary personal data; may constitute biometric-adjacent imagery of a child (not classified as Art. 9 biometric data unless used for unique identification, which the app does not do) |
| Preferences & travel | Personal preferences; visas, transit passes/Jahreskarte | Ordinary personal data |

### 2.3 Processing activities
1. **Manual entry / structured storage** — the user types or edits records directly.
2. **AI-assisted entry via OCR** — the user photographs a document; the AI assistant extracts structured fields from the image and (subject to user confirmation) files them into the relevant record.
3. **AI assistant Q&A** — the user asks questions about their stored family data; the assistant reads family context to answer.
4. **AI avatar restyling** — a photo is transformed into a stylised avatar image via AI image generation.
5. **Deterministic reminders/nudges** — non-AI, rule-based logic that surfaces upcoming expiries and check-up dates from stored data.
6. **Carer-share links** — an admin/member generates a time-limited link exposing a **server-side-whitelisted, carer-safe subset** (allergies, medications, conditions, and similar emergency-relevant fields only) to whoever holds the link, without requiring the carer to have an account.

### 2.4 Data flow across borders (this is the core transfer picture for this DPIA)

```

        │  HTTPS/TLS
        ▼
Firebase Authentication (Google Identity)  ─── may process in the UNITED STATES
        │                                       Safeguard: Google Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)

        │

        europe-west2, UNITED KINGDOM  ─── UK benefits from an EU adequacy decision
        │
        │  (family-context payload, minimised — see §6.5 — sent only when
```

### 4.2 Art. 6(1)(b) vs Art. 6(1)(f) — is "contract" the right basis at all?
Using Art. 6(1)(b) assumes there is a contract for a service the user requested. For a free/beta/friends-test product with no formal terms-of-service acceptance flow described in the fact set, this basis is plausible but not verified as correctly implemented (e.g., is there a clear point at which the user accepts terms describing this processing?). `{{PLACEHOLDER: confirm a terms-of-service / privacy-notice acceptance step exists and precedes account creation}}`

### 4.3 Art. 9(2)(a) explicit consent for health data — quality bar
"Explicit consent" under Art. 9 is a higher bar than ordinary Art. 6(1)(a) consent: it must be a clear affirmative act, specific to the health-data purpose, separate from other consents, informed (naming what will be processed, by whom, for how long), and freely withdrawable. The fact set confirms the gate is **off by default** and **in-app**, which is the right starting shape, but this draft cannot confirm from the facts alone that the consent copy itself meets the specificity/informed-ness bar, or that withdrawal actually purges previously AI-processed copies. `{{PLACEHOLDER: have the actual consent-screen copy and withdrawal mechanics reviewed against Art. 9(2)(a)/Art. 7 requirements}}`

### 4.4 Art. 8 children's data
The child does not consent to their own data — the parent enters and manages it on the child's behalf, consistent with the general reading of parental responsibility for a child's records within a family context. Two things this draft flags rather than resolves:
- **A child-role account can never enable AI features** (server-enforced) — this is a real, verifiable safeguard and should be called out positively in any lawyer review, not just as a risk mitigation.
- Austria's digital-consent age for a **child's own** information-society-service use is 14 (per existing project research in `legal-compliance-research-2026-07.md`), which is a different question from *parental* management of a child's records — the app's model (parent manages, child never independently consents to AI) sidesteps that age threshold for AI specifically, but does not by itself resolve whether a 14+ teenager who is a `member` (not `child`) role and personally uses AI features needs to be treated as consenting for themselves. `{{PLACEHOLDER: confirm the role model correctly maps "child who should not self-consent" to the child role, especially for teenagers who might otherwise be given member-level access}}`

## 5. Risks to data subjects

Each risk is rated **Likelihood × Severity → Overall**, on a Low/Medium/High scale, reflecting the pre-mitigation inherent risk. Residual risk after the measures in §6 is given at the end of each entry.

### 5.1 Breach of children's health data
- **Scenario:** A vulnerability, misconfigured access rule, or compromised admin credential exposes a child's allergy, medication, or chronic-condition data.
- **Likelihood:** Medium (the app is a plausible target precisely because it aggregates high-value data; solo-operator security operations have less redundancy than a team).
- **Severity:** High (Art. 9 data, minor data subject, potential for real-world harm — e.g., stalking, targeted social engineering, insurance discrimination, safeguarding concerns).
- **Overall (pre-mitigation): High.**
- **Residual after §6 measures (Firestore family-scoping rules, server-only role docs, HTTPS/TLS, AES-256-GCM for the credentials vault specifically):** Medium — the general family-record store (including health fields) is protected by access-control rules but, per the fact set, is **not** described as field-level encrypted the way the credentials vault is. That asymmetry is itself worth flagging: the most "obviously sensitive" data (passwords) got encryption; the Art. 9 health data did not, per the facts given. `{{PLACEHOLDER: confirm whether health-data fields warrant the same field-level encryption treatment as the credentials vault, or whether Firestore's platform-level encryption-at-rest plus access rules is judged sufficient}}`

### 5.2 Over-broad AI processing of sensitive data
- **Scenario:** The AI assistant is asked a question, and more family context is sent to Vertex AI than is strictly needed to answer it — e.g., a question about a school pickup time inadvertently includes health notes in the context payload.
- **Likelihood:** Medium (context-minimisation is described as implemented for passwords and raw document images specifically, but the fact set does not describe field-by-field minimisation logic for every AI call).
- **Severity:** Medium-High (Art. 9 data leaving the storage boundary more often than necessary, even to a same-jurisdiction, no-training processor, is a proportionality problem, not just a security one).
- **Overall (pre-mitigation): Medium-High.**
- **Residual after §6 measures (AI context minimisation stripping passwords/raw document images, off-by-default consent gate, child-role AI block, Vertex AI EU endpoint with no model-training use):** Medium — the named minimisation measures address the clearest over-collection vectors (secrets, raw images) but do not, per the facts given, describe minimisation of *which family members'* or *which category of* data is included in a given AI context window. `{{PLACEHOLDER: confirm whether AI context is scoped to only the data relevant to the specific question/record being worked on, or whether it is broader family-wide context by default}}`

### 5.3 Carer-share link exposure
- **Scenario:** A carer-share link is guessed, leaked (e.g., forwarded, screenshotted, left open on a shared device), or used beyond its intended window.
- **Likelihood:** Low-Medium (unguessable 24-byte tokens make brute-force effectively infeasible; the residual exposure vector is human — forwarding or device sharing — not cryptographic).
- **Severity:** Medium (the data exposed is deliberately narrowed to a carer-safe whitelist — allergies, medications, conditions — rather than the full record, which caps the blast radius, but this is still Art. 9 data reaching a party outside the family without an account or identity check).
- **Overall (pre-mitigation): Medium.**
- **Residual after §6 measures (unguessable 24-byte tokens, server-enforced 48h default/7-day max expiry, revocability, server-side field whitelist, HTML-escaping of output):** Low-Medium — the technical controls are solid; the remaining exposure is inherent to the "link = access" model (anyone who has the link has the data for its lifetime) and cannot be fully engineered away, only bounded. `{{PLACEHOLDER: confirm whether link creation/access is logged so a suspected leak can be investigated and the specific link revoked}}`

### 5.4 Unauthorised family access (cross-family or within-family privilege escalation)
- **Scenario:** A bug or misconfigured rule lets User A see or edit Family B's records, or lets a `child`/`member` role self-elevate to `admin`.
- **Likelihood:** Low, specifically because the fact set describes this as an already-addressed design point (Firestore rules enforce family-scoping; role/membership documents are server-only so no client-side self-assignment is possible), and prior project work (per repo history) shows active security hardening of exactly this class of issue.
- **Severity:** High if it occurred (a scoping bug in a multi-tenant family app is a full-family data breach, not a partial one).
- **Overall (pre-mitigation absent the stated controls): High; with the stated controls, treated as a Low-likelihood/High-severity → Medium overall.**
- **Residual after §6 measures:** Low-Medium — contingent on the Firestore rules being correctly deployed and periodically re-audited, since rules can regress with future feature changes. `{{PLACEHOLDER: establish a recurring (e.g. pre-release) Firestore/Storage rules review process, not a one-time audit}}`

### 5.5 Secret/credential exposure (passwords, wifi, door codes)
- **Scenario:** Database access (legitimate or via breach) exposes the household's passwords, wifi keys, or door codes in usable form.
- **Likelihood:** Low for the encrypted vault itself given AES-256-GCM with a KMS-backed key (an attacker with database read access alone cannot decrypt without the key); Medium if the threat model includes compromise of the server/application layer that holds decryption capability at runtime.
- **Severity:** High (physical-security implications — door codes — compound the usual credential-reuse risk).
- **Overall (pre-mitigation): High.**
- **Residual after §6 measures (AES-256-GCM field encryption, Secret-Manager-backed key, AI context minimisation explicitly stripping passwords before any AI call):** Low-Medium. This is the best-protected data category in the app per the facts given. The residual risk is concentrated in key-management operational hygiene (who can access the Secret Manager key, is it rotated, are Cloud Run service-account permissions minimal) rather than the cryptographic design itself. `{{PLACEHOLDER: document key-rotation policy and confirm least-privilege IAM on the Secret Manager key and the Cloud Run service account}}`

### 5.6 International-transfer risk (Firebase Authentication in the US)
- **Scenario:** Authentication data (at minimum: email, sign-in metadata, auth tokens) processed by Firebase Authentication is transferred to/processed in the United States, a jurisdiction without a current EU adequacy decision, relying instead on Google's SCCs.
- **Likelihood:** High that the transfer *occurs* (it is architectural, not incidental) — the question is whether the safeguard is adequate, not whether the transfer happens.
- **Severity:** Low-Medium — the specific data category touching the US (authentication metadata) is narrower and less sensitive than the family-record content, which stays in the UK/EU per the fact set; but any Art. 9/Art. 8 (children's) data that ever touches US-processed auth metadata (e.g. if a child's email were ever used for their own login) would raise severity.
- **Overall (pre-mitigation): Medium.**
- **Residual after §6 measures (SCCs as the transfer safeguard; the deliberate architectural choice to keep Firestore/Storage/Cloud Run/Vertex AI in the UK/EU and use the US-touching service only for the narrow authentication function):** Low-Medium — SCCs are a recognised Art. 46 safeguard, but post-*Schrems II* case law expects a transfer-impact assessment (TIA) alongside SCCs, particularly given US government surveillance access considerations for a US-headquartered processor. This draft does not include a TIA. `{{PLACEHOLDER: complete a transfer-impact assessment (TIA) for the Firebase Authentication US transfer, or evaluate switching to an EU-only identity provider if a TIA cannot be satisfactorily completed}}`
